# Steak Diane

A quick-sear tenderloin with a pan sauce built on shallots, mushrooms, cognac, Dijon, Worcestershire, and cream. Named for Diana, Roman goddess of the hunt; born as a tableside flambé at a London hotel in 1936 and recognizable in that lineage ever since.

Serves 4.

## Ingredients

### For the steaks

- 4 beef tenderloin medallions (filet mignon), 6 oz each and about 1½ inches thick
- 1 teaspoon kosher salt (for dry brining)
- Freshly cracked black pepper
- 1 tablespoon neutral high-smoke-point oil (grapeseed, avocado, or clarified butter)

### For the sauce

- 3 tablespoons unsalted butter, divided (1 tablespoon for the pan, 2 tablespoons cold cubed for finishing)
- 1 large shallot, finely minced (about 3 tablespoons)
- 8 oz cremini or baby bella mushrooms, sliced ¼-inch thick
- 2 cloves garlic, finely minced
- ¼ cup cognac or brandy
- 1 cup beef stock (homemade, or Better Than Bouillon)
- 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard
- 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
- ⅓ cup heavy cream
- 1 tablespoon fresh parsley, finely chopped
- 1 tablespoon fresh chives, thinly sliced
- Juice of ¼ lemon (optional, for brightness)
- Kosher salt and freshly cracked black pepper, to taste

## Instructions

### Prep (ideally 40 minutes to 24 hours ahead)

- Pat the steaks dry with paper towels and season all over with the 1 teaspoon kosher salt
- Set the steaks on a wire rack over a plate and refrigerate uncovered — this is a dry brine; it seasons the meat deeply and dries the surface for a better crust
- Pull the steaks from the fridge 30 minutes before cooking so they come close to room temperature
- Pat dry one more time and season generously with cracked black pepper right before searing

### Cook

- Have every ingredient measured and within reach before the pan goes on the heat — once the steaks hit the pan the dish moves fast and there is no time to measure Worcestershire mid-cook
- Heat a 12-inch heavy skillet (cast iron or stainless) over medium-high heat for 2 minutes until shimmering, then add the oil
- Sear the steaks undisturbed for 2 to 3 minutes per side, until a deep brown crust forms and the internal temperature reaches 125°F for medium-rare
- Transfer the steaks to a warm plate and tent loosely with foil — they will finish cooking from residual heat and go back in the pan at the end
- Reduce heat to medium, add 1 tablespoon of butter and the minced shallot, and sauté 1 minute until fragrant and translucent
- Add the mushrooms with a pinch of salt and spread them in a single layer; leave them undisturbed for 2 to 3 minutes to begin browning, then stir and continue cooking 4 to 5 minutes more until deeply golden and all the liquid has evaporated
- Add the garlic and cook 30 seconds — do not let it brown
- Pull the pan off the heat and add the cognac (safety: pouring alcohol onto a hot pan near a flame can ignite — move off the burner first)
- Flambé optional: tilt the pan toward a gas flame or use a long match to ignite the fumes, then let the flames burn out (15 to 30 seconds); if skipping, return the pan to the heat and reduce the alcohol for 1 to 2 minutes
- Add the beef stock, Dijon mustard, and Worcestershire sauce; whisk together and scrape up the browned bits from the bottom of the pan
- Simmer briskly for 3 to 4 minutes until the liquid reduces by about half
- Reduce heat to medium-low, stir in the heavy cream, and simmer gently for 2 minutes until the sauce coats the back of a spoon
- Off the heat, add the 2 tablespoons of cold cubed butter one piece at a time, swirling the pan to emulsify — this is the mount, and it gives the sauce its glossy restaurant finish; do not boil again after mounting
- Return the steaks to the pan along with any resting juices on the plate; spoon sauce over the top and warm through for 30 to 60 seconds
- Taste the sauce: add lemon juice if it is heavy, more salt if flat, more pepper if dull
- Transfer the steaks to warm plates, spoon the sauce generously over the top, and finish with the parsley and chives
- Serve immediately

## Serving Suggestions

The sauce is rich and the steak is quick, so pair with one starch and one sharp green:

- Starch: [crispy roasted potatoes](../sides/crispy-roasted-potatoes.md), buttery mashed potatoes, or buttered egg noodles — the traditional French bistro pairing
- Green: [sautéed green beans](../sides/sauteed-green-beans.md), roasted asparagus, or wilted spinach — the acid and bite cut the cream
- Also good: [roasted carrots](../sides/roasted-carrots.md) finished with a splash of sherry vinegar
- A simple butter-lettuce salad with lemon vinaigrette if you want brightness on the plate
- Wine: a full-bodied red — Cabernet Sauvignon, a right-bank Bordeaux, or a California Zinfandel — holds up to the pan sauce without fighting the beef

## Substitutions

- Steak cut: NY strip or ribeye pounded to ½ inch for more beef flavor and less tenderness; sirloin for the traditional 1936 version. Avoid flank or skirt — too fibrous for a pan sauce.
- Cognac / brandy: dry sherry, Madeira, or dry Marsala. In a pinch, dry white wine (reduce more aggressively — it has less depth).
- Beef stock: half beef and half chicken for a lighter sauce; homemade is better; Better Than Bouillon beats most boxed broths.
- Dijon: whole-grain mustard adds texture; 1 teaspoon dry mustard works and is closer to the 1953 New York variation.
- Mushrooms: shiitake (stems removed), oyster, chanterelle, or a wild blend for more depth. Button mushrooms work but are less flavorful. Omit entirely for a leaner, more classical sauce.
- Cream: crème fraîche gives a slight tang; omit entirely for a cleaner pan-reduction closer to the 1936 original.

## Storage

- This dish does not reheat well. The sauce separates and the steak overcooks. Plan to eat it fresh.
- Sauce only: refrigerate up to 2 days. Re-emulsify by warming gently with 1 tablespoon water and whisking in 1 tablespoon cold butter.
- Leftover sliced steak: eat cold on a salad or in a sandwich with horseradish — do not reheat it.

## Notes

- Mise en place is non-negotiable. From the first sear to the finished sauce is about 15 minutes with no stopping points.
- The dry brine is the single biggest upgrade over most Steak Diane recipes. Even 40 minutes helps; overnight is noticeably better.
- A properly rested steak releases pink juices onto the plate — pour them back into the sauce at the end. That is the shortest path to restaurant depth.
- If the sauce breaks (butter and cream separate), remove from heat and vigorously whisk in 1 tablespoon of cold cream.
- Steak Diane began as a tableside flambé showpiece. Keep the flames if you have an audience; skip them if you are cooking alone — they add nothing you can taste.
