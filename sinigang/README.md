# Pork Sinigang (Sinigang na Baboy)

Serves 4 to 6.

Sinigang is the sour-forward Filipino pork soup — an assertive tamarind broth built on bone-in pork, tomatoes, and a staged sequence of vegetables. The whole dish hinges on two things: a clean, skimmed broth and vegetables added in the right order so nothing goes mushy.

## Ingredients

### Pork and broth

- 2 pounds Pork spare ribs, cut into 2-inch pieces (or 1 pound ribs + 1 pound pork belly for a richer broth)
- 8 cups Water (about 2 quarts)
- 1 medium Yellow onion, quartered
- 3 medium Roma tomatoes, quartered (reserve 1 tomato for the end)
- 2 tablespoons Fish sauce (patis), plus more to taste
- 1/2 teaspoon Black pepper

### Souring agent — pick one

- 4 tablespoons Tamarind paste or concentrate (easiest — Whole Foods, Trader Joe's, or Middle Eastern aisle carry it)
- 1 to 2 packets Knorr Sinigang sa Sampalok mix, 20 to 40 g total (the Filipino weeknight default — Amazon or any Asian aisle)
- 1 pound Fresh green tamarind pods (sampalok), if you can find them at a Filipino market — traditional and the best flavor

### Vegetables

- 2 pieces Taro root (gabi), peeled and halved — substitute 2 medium Yukon Gold potatoes if unavailable
- 1 medium Daikon radish (labanos), peeled and cut into 1/2-inch half-moons — white turnip works in a pinch
- 1 medium Chinese or Japanese eggplant, cut into 1-inch half-moons — Italian eggplant works, cut smaller since the skin is tougher
- 8 to 10 Chinese long beans (sitaw), cut into 2-inch pieces — regular green beans or haricots verts are a fine substitute
- 6 to 8 Okra pods, trimmed
- 2 to 3 Long green chilies (siling haba), left whole — substitute Anaheim or banana peppers for mild heat, poblano for more
- 1 bunch Water spinach (kangkong), leaves and stems separated — substitute regular spinach or baby spinach; add off-heat either way
- 1 tablespoon Kosher salt, to taste

## Instructions

- Rinse the pork under cold water and place in a wide, heavy pot with the 8 cups of water — a wide pot makes skimming much easier
- Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to a bare simmer
- Skim aggressively for the first 15 minutes with a slotted spoon or fine strainer, removing all the grey foam and scum that rises — this is the single most important step for a clean broth, do not stir it back in
- Once the broth runs clear, add the onion, 2 of the quartered tomatoes, 1 tablespoon of the fish sauce, and the black pepper
- Cover partially and simmer gently for 60 to 90 minutes, until the pork is fork-tender and pulls easily from the bone — keep the heat low, a rolling boil will cloud the broth
- Add the taro (gabi) and simmer for 5 minutes — if you want a thicker, more stew-like broth, mash 1 or 2 pieces against the side of the pot as they soften
- Add the tamarind paste or Knorr mix and stir to dissolve
- Add the daikon and eggplant and simmer for 3 minutes
- Add the long beans, okra, long chilies, and the reserved tomato wedges — simmer for 2 minutes
- Add the kangkong stems and simmer for 1 minute
- Turn off the heat, add the kangkong leaves (or spinach) and stir once — residual heat wilts them in 2 minutes without turning them grey
- Taste and adjust: more fish sauce for salt and depth, more tamarind for sour, a pinch of sugar if you overshot the sour, more water if the broth got too concentrated
- Serve immediately in deep bowls with steamed white rice on the side

## Serving Suggestions

Serve with any combination of:

- Steamed jasmine or long-grain white rice — traditional and non-negotiable, the rice absorbs the sour broth
- A small dish of extra fish sauce with a sliced Thai chili for dipping the pork
- Sliced calamansi or lime wedges for those who want more sour at the table
- A cold San Miguel or a light lager

## Substitutions

- Pork: pork shoulder (kasim) works but the broth is thinner without bone — bump the aromatics if using; avoid loin or chops, they are too lean
- Souring agent: fresh kamias (bilimbi), green mango, or guava (bayabas) all produce recognizable regional variants — guava gives a milder, slightly sweet dish; kamias gives a sharper, brighter one
- Kangkong: spinach is the closest common-store substitute; watercress works too; skip bok choy — it takes longer to cook and changes the character
- Taro (gabi): Yukon Gold potato is the best substitute — russets fall apart, sweet potatoes throw off the flavor
- Long green chili: Anaheim (mild), banana pepper (mildest), or poblano (some heat) — jalapeño works but is noticeably spicier
- Daikon: white turnip, or omit if unavailable — the dish survives without it
- Calamansi (for finishing acid): 50/50 lime and lemon juice

## Storage

- Refrigerator: up to 3 days in an airtight container — the sour deepens overnight, many people prefer day-two sinigang
- Freezer: up to 2 months, but the vegetables lose texture on thaw — freeze broth and pork only if you plan ahead
- Reheating: warm gently on the stovetop over low heat; if you froze the broth, add fresh vegetables when reheating for the best texture

## Notes

- Do not sauté the aromatics or brown the pork — this is a pure-boil dish, and searing muddies the clean sour broth that defines sinigang
- Skimming is not optional; the difference between a clear broth and a cloudy one is 10 minutes of attention in the first quarter hour
- Kangkong (or spinach) leaves go in after the heat is off — every failed sinigang has grey, overcooked greens
- Sinigang should be assertively sour, not "hinting at" sour — if you're unsure, add half a Knorr packet or 2 more tablespoons of tamarind paste and taste before committing
- If you oversour, a small pinch of sugar or a splash more water rescues it
- Gabi mashed into the broth is a widespread family variation — decide which version you're making before you start cooking

## Variations

- **Sinigang sa miso**: whisk 2 tablespoons of white miso paste into the broth with the tamarind — common with pork and adds umami depth
- **Sinigang sa bayabas** (guava): replace tamarind with 4 to 6 ripe guavas, mashed and strained — a milder, slightly sweet dish with a distinct character
- **Sinigang sa kamias**: replace tamarind with 1 cup of kamias (bilimbi) fruit, boiled and mashed — sharper and brighter than tamarind
- **Gabi-thickened**: mash all 2 pieces of taro into the broth mid-cook for a light stew consistency instead of a clear soup
